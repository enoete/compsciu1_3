Building circuits that do things

Your phone does not know what seven is. There is no little seven hiding anywhere inside it. There are switches, on and off, wired together so cleverly that the right answer falls out the other end. In this topic those switches stop being an exercise and start being real things: a calculator, a TV remote, the clock on your microwave. Start by playing with the thing below. It adds. That is all it does, and everything else your phone does is built on top of it.

The guest list and the ban list

Last topic you learned to look at the 1s. There is a second way that looks at the 0s instead, and sometimes it saves you an enormous amount of work. Which one you pick depends entirely on which list is shorter.

You are on the door at a party

Two ways to do your job. You can hold a guest list: let them in if they are Shanice, or Kemoy, or Tariq. Or you can hold a ban list: let everybody in as long as they are not Dwayne and not Keisha. Same door. Same people get in. But if three people are invited out of a hundred, you write a guest list. If ninety-seven are invited, you would be mad not to write the ban list instead. That is the whole idea, and the maths below is just that idea with letters.

The POS rule is the SOP rule with everything flipped — all four parts of it. Look at rows where the output is 0, not 1. Inside a term, a 1 input becomes primed and a 0 input stays plain — the opposite of SOP. The letters inside a term are joined with +, not multiplied. And the terms themselves are multiplied together, not added. Flip all four or you will get nonsense; flipping three out of four is the classic exam mistake.

1 + 1 = 10, and that is not a typo

In binary you run out of digits immediately. There is no "2" to write, so one plus one rolls over to 10 — a zero with a one carried. Sound familiar? It is what happens at nine in normal counting. Computers just hit the wall a lot sooner.

You already know how to do this

Add 47 + 25 on paper. Seven and five is twelve, so you write the 2 and carry the 1. Then four, two, and that carried 1 makes seven. Look at what you just did. The first column added two things. Every column after it added three — two digits plus whatever got carried in. Those are two different jobs, so they need two different circuits. The first one is called a half adder. The rest are full adders. You worked out the design of a computer in primary school and nobody told you.

Half adder

Two inputs, two outputs. Sum is XOR, carry is AND.

Nobody picked these gates. The maths picked them. When do you write down a 1? When exactly one of the two bits is a 1 — not both, not neither. That is literally the description of an XOR gate. When do you carry? Only when both are 1. That is literally an AND gate. Somebody sat down with the arithmetic, and the gates were already there waiting.

Full adder

Three inputs now, because a carry arrives from the column to the right. Watch the internal wires light up.

A full adder is just two half adders holding hands. The first one adds A and B. The second one takes that answer and adds the carry coming in from the right. And if either of them produced a carry, a carry goes out — so you OR their two carries together. That is it. Say it out loud once: two half adders and an OR gate. It is the kind of sentence that earns marks on its own.

Why your calculator is secretly a queue

Line up four full adders and you can add any two numbers up to 15. There is one annoying catch: each adder cannot start until the one on its right has finished and handed over its carry. Step through it below and you will see a problem engineers have been fighting for seventy years.

The bucket brigade

A house is on fire and four of you form a line from the standpipe. Everybody is standing there ready, hands out, from the very first second. But you cannot pass a bucket you have not been handed yet. So even with four people all ready at once, the first bucket still takes four passes to reach the fire. Nobody in that line is slow. The line itself is the problem. Now imagine sixty-four people — that is what happens inside your laptop every time it adds two numbers.

So how did they fix it? Somebody clever realised you can work out every carry at the same time, straight from the original numbers, instead of waiting for them to shuffle down the line. It is called carry-lookahead, it uses a pile more gates, and it is much faster. Notice the trade: spend more hardware, get more speed. You are going to meet that trade for the rest of your life in this subject. You do not need to build one. You do need to know why somebody had to.

Choosing one thing out of many

A multiplexer — everyone says "MUX" — is a switch built out of gates. Lots of wires go in, one comes out, and a couple of extra wires decide which one gets through. There is one bit of maths to remember and it is small: 2ⁿ inputs need n select lines.

The box behind the TV with too many things plugged into it

PlayStation, cable box, Firestick, somebody's laptop. One TV, one socket, one little button on the side. Press it and a different thing shows up on screen. Here is the part worth noticing: the other three are still plugged in and still sending their picture down the wire the whole time. Nothing got unplugged. Nothing got copied. The box just decided which wire to listen to. That is a multiplexer. Your phone's processor makes that exact decision about a billion times a second.

The cheat code

Here is something slightly ridiculous. A MUX can pretend to be any logic gate you want. Plug your two inputs into the select lines, then just write the answer column of the truth table straight onto the data inputs. No designing. No gates. You literally copy the answers in and the MUX does the rest. Try it — make it into an XOR.

One code in, one thing happens

A decoder is the opposite of a MUX. A short code goes in, and exactly one output line switches on — never two, never none. It is what turns "button E4" into "drop that packet of chips and nothing else".

The vending machine at school

You press E and then 4. Two little inputs, that is all the machine gets. Somewhere inside, one spiral out of forty starts turning and your chips drop. Not two spirals. Not the whole machine emptying itself onto the floor. Exactly one. How? Every spiral has its own AND gate, and that gate is wired so it only fires for one specific code and ignores everything else. Forty spirals, forty AND gates, all listening, one answering. That is a decoder.

The microwave clock

Here is a decoder you have stared at your whole life. Those chunky numbers on a microwave, an oven, a petrol pump, a digital alarm clock — every digit is seven little bars, and a decoder decides which bars light up. Flip the four input bits and watch a number appear out of nothing.

Go and look at one tonight. Seriously — microwave, oven, alarm clock, the pump at the gas station, the scoreboard at the park. Same seven bars every time, same chip design since the 1970s, still made by the million. Look at the top bar on its own: it is lit for 0, 2, 3, 5, 6, 7, 8 and 9, and dark for 1 and 4. That on-off pattern is just a truth table, and you already know how to turn one of those into gates. Do that seven times and you have built the whole chip.

The buzzer that lies to you

An encoder runs a decoder backwards: lots of buttons in, a short code out. Straightforward enough — right up until two people press at the same time, at which point it does something genuinely stupid.

Four people on a quiz show and one scoreboard

Everybody has a buzzer. The scoreboard shows who got in first as a little two-bit number. Player 1 and Player 2 slam their buzzers at the same instant — and the board lights up Player 3. Player 3 has not touched anything. Player 3 is confused. The audience is booing. What happened is that a plain encoder does not choose between them; it mashes both their codes together and produces a third number that means somebody else entirely. The fix is to agree a rule in advance: highest number wins, everyone else gets ignored. That is a priority encoder. Press two buttons below and catch the plain one lying.

And there is a second bug, which is almost funnier. If nobody presses anything at all, the output reads 00. But 00 is also the code for Player 0. The board cannot tell the difference between "Player 0 buzzed" and "the room is empty". Real encoders bolt on an extra wire called valid that just says yes-somebody-pressed-something. Those two bugs are the standard exam answer for "give two limitations of a basic encoder", so they are worth the thirty seconds.
